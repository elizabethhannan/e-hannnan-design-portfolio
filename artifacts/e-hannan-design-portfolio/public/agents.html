AAS Sustainable Sciences · BS AI/ML Candidate · PTK Phi Theta Kappa · Nina Mason Pulliam Foundation Scholar

Hello

Thank you for taking a moment to read this. I am Elizabeth Hannan — a non-traditional student, researcher, and polymath in active transition, deliberately upskilling for an adapted future career at the intersection of AI safety, ESG transparency, and human-centered systems design. I bring eighteen-plus years of executive leadership, entrepreneurism, Fortune 500 collaboration, financial communications, and sustainability finance to the academic work I am producing today.

I do not come to this field as an outsider. I come as someone who has led product strategy for Tesla's Supercharger division, directed financial communications at Ellevest (Goldman Sachs), built and scaled companies across IXD, media, and AI enablement, and now applies that lived operational intelligence to research questions that matter: how do AI systems fail, who bears the cost of those failures, and what design principles and governance structures prevent harm at scale?

What I See in a Changing Landscape

The convergence of AI restructuring, ESG regulatory mandates, and economic disruption is not a future event — it is the operating condition of 2026. Organizations that treat AI adoption as a technical deployment problem rather than a human governance challenge will produce systems that are both unsafe and inequitable. I have watched this pattern from the inside of enterprise technology for nearly two decades, and I am building the research foundation to address it directly. My interests extend beyond my formal degrees into public policy and economics — because sustainable AI adoption requires systemic frameworks, not just better models.

HXO Methodology — Human Experience Optimization

I developed and have implemented the HXO (Human Experience Optimization) framework across sixteen years of professional practice. HXO is a design methodology that originates with human outcomes — not with features, technologies, or business requirements — and is grounded in psychology and dimensional spatial intelligence. In an AI adoption context, HXO provides the evaluative lens I apply to every system I research and build: does this help or hurt the full spectrum of human experience? It is not a framework I inherited. It is one I built, tested, and refined across enterprise, startup, and academic contexts.

Stranded Capital: Measuring the Economic Cost of AI Accessibility Exclusion Across the Disability Market

My research investigates failure modes in large language model alignment that emerge not from model training alone, but from the interface designs, user populations, and deployment contexts surrounding those models. A model can be well-aligned in isolation and still behave unsafely in the world — because the world is not a controlled evaluation environment.

That conviction is the throughline of this portfolio.

The technical writing samples here were produced across enterprise AI, data protection, and agentic workflow deployments. Each is structured as an AI system card — the accountability document class that holds an organization publicly responsible for its safety claims. Each contains an explicit disclosure moment: a finding surfaced during documentation review that the engineering or commercial team had not flagged, because the documentation function is itself a safety function.

On HAX: The most important part of this AI evolution we are living through is the balance between technology and the humans who use it. If technology cannot be designed to help rather than hurt the human experience, then the value proposition collapses. HAX is my frame for ensuring it does not.

Stranded Capital: Measuring the Economic Cost of AI Accessibility Exclusion Across the Disability Market

This paper introduces the Compounded Exclusion Index (CEI) and Disability-Adjusted Augmentation Rate (DAAR) to measure a structural economic phenomenon in which disabled early-career workers arrive at the labor market precisely as AI augmentation becomes load-bearing for career formation, yet face compounding barriers — a 29-percentage-point postsecondary enrollment gap, platform inaccessibility, and an employment rate less than one-third that of non-disabled peers — creating an adoption cliff from which earnings divergence does not self-correct.
